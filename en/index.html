LTG – Lifecycle Tax Graph

Concept and case study

v0.2 · 29 October 2026 · working draft

Every tax can be reduced to two events – possession (stock) and movement (flow) – and every asset can be described as a state machine in which each transition carries a tax rule.

1.The problem

Today’s tax and registration systems are text-centric. Rules live scattered across statutes, annexes and amendments. The same asset – a car, for instance – passes through several tax types over its lifetime: VAT, registration tax, motor vehicle tax, transaction duty. The rules describe separately what happens on purchase, sale, theft, export and scrapping.

This makes four things hard:

  • answering what tax is due in a concrete case,
  • simulating the effect of a reform,
  • making the body of law machine-readable,
  • comparing countries.

LTG introduces no new tax. It provides a new way of describing the existing rules. Legal force continues to come from the underlying statute; LTG merely represents the same rule in a single, verifiable model.

2.The core model

LTG rests on two primitives:

  • STOCK – possession of the object at a point in time; typically an annual tax.
  • FLOW – transfer of the object; typically a transaction tax.

A third, non-tax element joins them: the state. The object’s lifecycle is a finite state machine:

NEW ──BUY──► IN_CIRCULATION ──SELL──► IN_CIRCULATION
                 │
                 ├── THEFT ─────► STOLEN ──RECOVERY──► IN_CIRCULATION
                 │                   └── not recovered ──► LOSS
                 ├── TOTAL_LOSS ─► LOSS
                 ├── EXPORT ─────► EXPORTED
                 └── SCRAP ──────► SCRAPPED

Every arrow is a transition, and every transition carries a tax rule. The rule has these fields:

FieldMeaning
basewhat the tax applies to (price, value, power)
ratepercentage or fixed amount
payerwho is legally liable
bearerwho actually bears it
duewhen it becomes due
exemptionexceptions
sanctionwhat happens on non-payment

The notation used for references in this document:

LTG:CAR.IN_CIRCULATION --SELL--> LTG:CAR.IN_CIRCULATION [16%, seller]
LTG:CAR.IN_CIRCULATION --THEFT--> LTG:CAR.STOLEN [0%, no tax]
LTG:CAR.STOLEN --RECOVERY--> LTG:CAR.IN_CIRCULATION [0%, no tax]

3.1. Theft and related scenarios

Theft is not a taxable event in itself: there is no consideration and no transaction. But it changes the state, and from that point every further event follows a different rule. Five cases matter in practice:

#ScenarioTaxWho bears the risk
1Sold but not yet re-registered, then stolen0% (registration did not occur)the buyer, under the rules of performance
2Stolen, then sold by the thief0% (the transaction is void)the thief; a good-faith buyer does not acquire title to stolen property
3Stolen, not recovered, insurer pays0% (compensation, not a tax base)the insurer
4Recovered, then sold16% (ordinary sale)the buyer
5Taken abroad0%, pro-rata refund of the purchase taxthe destination country taxes it

The key design decision is what happens to the purchase tax already paid when the car is stolen. There are three options: refund, credit against the next car, or forfeiture. The concept recommends a credit, because it does not encourage staged thefts and does not burden the budget with immediate refunds.

4.Used cars

“Used” is not a lifecycle state but an attribute. Treating it as a state would explode the graph: USED, USED_STOLEN, USED_LOSS, USED_EXPORTED and so on. LTG therefore keeps it as an attribute:

LTG:CAR
  condition = new | used
  age       = 0..N years
  owner     = private | company | insurer

The graph stays the same; only the rate is parameterised:

StateconditionExample rate (buy / sell)
NEWnew17% / 16%
IN_CIRCULATIONused, 0–3 years14% / 13%
IN_CIRCULATIONused, 3–8 years11% / 10%
IN_CIRCULATIONused, 8+ years7% / 6%

The figures are illustrative; final rates require simulation. The point is the structure: one graph with parameterised rates, not a separate state machine for every age bracket.

5.Enforcement

“Non-payment → 1–3 years in prison or USD 10,000” is disproportionate and expensive. Prison costs millions of forints per year, while the debt is often smaller. Instead, an automatic, graduated ladder:

0. automatic assessment
1. 8-day reminder
2. 30-day instalment plan
3. lien / wage garnishment
4. suspension of registration or operating licence
5. asset freeze
6. criminal stage: only for intentional concealment, above a high threshold

Prison thus becomes the sanction for fraud, not for tax debt. This is more defensible legally, cheaper, and protects revenue better, because asset enforcement is faster than criminal proceedings.

6.Structuring the body of law

The entire legal code cannot be compressed into a single state machine: much of law consists of definitions, principles, procedures and institutions, not events. Five layers are needed:

LayerFormatContent
1. TextAkoma Ntoso XMLsection, paragraph, amendment, structure
2. NormJSON-LDactor, act, condition, consequence
3. Event–stateLTG JSONstates, transitions, tax rules
4. SanctionJSONenforcement ladder, thresholds
5. Referencegraph (RDF / Neo4j)statute → statute

XML is not obsolete: Akoma Ntoso (OASIS LegalDocML) is the international standard for legislation. It is not, however, the right format for the event graph. The text therefore lives in XML, the semantics in JSON-LD, and the two are linked by references.

A transition in JSON-LD:

{
  "@type": "LTGTransition",
  "object": "LTG:CAR",
  "from": "IN_CIRCULATION",
  "event": "SELL",
  "to": "IN_CIRCULATION",
  "condition": { "condition": "used" },
  "tax": { "base": "price", "rate": 0.13, "payer": "seller" }
}

The norm layer extracts the quadruple actor + event + condition + consequence from the text. This can be done semi-automatically with a language model; the local ClauseLM is a good candidate. A lawyer must validate the extraction, because a misunderstood condition produces the wrong tax.

The model can be extended to several countries through a meta-schema:

The challenge is not the schema but legal language, conceptual differences and data licences. It is therefore best to start with HU, then EU law, then a second country (AT or DE).

9.Life of a car – a worked example

Take a new car priced at HUF 10 million. The buyer purchases it, sells it two years later, it is stolen, recovered, and sold again four years later.

#EventPrice / valueRateTaxPayer
1BUY (new)10,000,00017%1,700,000buyer
2SELL (used, 2 years)8,000,00013%1,040,000seller
3THEFT–0%0–
4RECOVERY–0%0–
5SELL (used, 4 years)6,000,00010%600,000seller
–Total––3,340,000–

The tax collected over the lifecycle is 33.4% of the initial price. By comparison, today’s 27% VAT at first sale is HUF 2.7 million, while used sales are practically tax-free. LTG therefore does not necessarily increase the total burden – it redistributes it along the lifecycle. The actual effect depends on the elasticity of transaction volumes: if the 13–17% rates reduce sales, both revenue and fleet composition change.

10.Risks

  • Legal validation: every extracted norm must be checked by a lawyer.
  • Data quality: the source corpus is fragmentary; missing sections can give a false picture.
  • Behavioural effect: high transaction rates may suppress the legal market.
  • Data licensing: each country’s corpus comes with different terms.
  • Change management: legislation changes constantly; the graph must be versioned.

11.Summary

LTG is not legislation but a description layer: it represents the lifecycle of assets as a state machine, and taxes as rules attached to transitions between states. The same model handles new and used cars, theft and export, Hungarian and German rates. The legal text continues to live in XML, the semantics in JSON-LD, and references connect the two.

The next step is the CAR pilot: processing 20–50 provisions, drawing the full event matrix and simulating the rates. If that works, the pattern can be extended to the full corpus and to multiple countries.
